# The "Stop" Exercise as Defined by Gurdjieff

The "stop" exercise is one of Gurdjieff's best-known practical techniques for self-observation and self-remembering, used in his Fourth Way work.

The mechanics are simple: at an unpredictable moment, the teacher (or someone appointed) calls out "Stop!" and every student must instantly freeze — not just stopping movement, but arresting everything exactly as it is: the position of the body, the facial expression, the direction of the gaze, the tension in the muscles, even the flow of thought and the breath as far as possible. The student holds this frozen state until released, however awkward or uncomfortable the posture.

The purpose behind it runs deeper than discipline. Gurdjieff taught that human beings live in a limited repertoire of automatic postures — physical, emotional, and mental — and that each habitual posture is bound up with habitual thoughts and feelings. We move from one automatic posture to another without ever occupying the transitional moments in between. The stop exercise interrupts this automatism mid-stream, trapping the student in an *unaccustomed* position that their machinery has no ready response to. In that gap, several things become possible: the student can observe their body, feelings, and thoughts as they actually are rather than as habit presents them; the ordinary link between posture and associated inner state is broken, allowing a taste of genuine self-observation; and the effort of holding the freeze while remaining inwardly awake becomes an exercise in will and self-remembering rather than mere physical endurance.

Gurdjieff was emphatic that the exercise only works when the command comes from outside and cannot be anticipated — if you stop yourself, you unconsciously prepare for it, and the machinery adjusts in advance. He also insisted it demanded unconditional obedience: you freeze regardless of what you're doing. Ouspensky recounts in *In Search of the Miraculous* a famous episode in Essentuki where a student crossing a canal froze on command while standing in rising water, staying put until Gurdjieff released him — illustrating (somewhat dramatically) the seriousness with which the exercise was treated.

In short: it's an externally-triggered, total freeze of body and inner state, designed to catch the human machine between its habitual postures so that, for a moment, one can actually see oneself and be present.
